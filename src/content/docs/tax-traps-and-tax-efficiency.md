There are some levels of earnings where disproportionately high amounts of
tax are paid due to the removal of allowances.

This wiki page outlines methods you might use to reduce your tax liability
on your income as well as some of your responsibilities, including whether
you need to submit a self assessment to HMRC.

:::note[About the figures on this page]
Figures are for the **2026/27 tax year**, which runs from 6 April 2026 to 5
April 2027. Rates and thresholds are for England, Wales and Northern Ireland
unless stated otherwise. Scottish taxpayers pay different rates of income
tax, and there is [a separate section below](#a-note-for-scottish-taxpayers).
Last checked 9 August 2026.
:::

## What is a 'tax trap'?

Generally it has been considered that there are two key tax traps:

- The progressive removal of the personal allowance on earnings between
  £100,000 and £125,140. Parents also lose Tax-Free Childcare and the
  funded childcare hours for working parents.
- The progressive removal of child benefit on earnings between £60,000 and
  £80,000. Following the changes made to the child benefit system for the
  2024/25 tax year, this trap is much less steep than it once was.

Income inside these bands is therefore taxed at a higher marginal rate.

There is a third trap that affects far fewer people but costs them a great
deal: the tapered annual allowance, which cuts how much you can put into a
pension once your income passes £200,000.

Note: unless we say otherwise, the marginal rates on this page refer to
income tax only and do not include National Insurance contributions. Where
the National Insurance position changes the answer materially we give both.
See our [Income Tax - The Basics](/income-tax/) primer for more on how the
bands work.

One thing worth knowing about all of these thresholds. The personal
allowance of £12,570 and the higher rate threshold of £50,270 are frozen
until 5 April 2031, as are the equivalent National Insurance thresholds
([HM Treasury, 26 November 2025](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/maintaining-income-tax-and-equivalent-national-insurance-contributions-thresholds-until-5-april-2031)).
The £100,000 and £60,000 trap thresholds are not uprated either. As wages
rise, more people are pulled into these bands each year.

## What is 'Adjusted Net Income'? {#Adjusted_Net_Income}

Your "adjusted net income" is your total taxable income from all sources,
before your personal allowance is applied, less certain reliefs. HMRC sets
out [the full four-step calculation](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/adjusted-net-income),
but the short version is that you add up everything taxable and then take
off:

- pension contributions you paid gross, without tax relief having been given (this is called a Net Pay arrangement and only relates to workplace pensions)
- pension contributions where your provider claimed basic rate relief for
  you, at the **grossed-up** amount. (For every £1 you paid in, take off
  £1.25) (this is called relief at source, and is the case for all personal pensions and many workplace pensions)
- Gift Aid donations, again at the grossed-up amount. For every £1 you
  donated, take off £1.25
- trading and property loss reliefs

Taxable income here means income from all sources, so salary, bonus, rental
income, and interest and dividends held outside an ISA. Even if that income
falls inside an allowance and no tax is actually paid on it, it still counts
towards your adjusted net income.

HMRC uses adjusted net income to work out whether your personal allowance is
reduced and whether you owe the High Income Child Benefit Charge. It is also
the measure used for Tax-Free Childcare and the funded childcare hours. So
if you can bring your adjusted net income down far enough, you escape these
traps.

:::note[Don't get caught out!]
Two things catch people out. Despite the name, adjusted net income is a
measure of income before tax, not your take-home pay after income tax and
National Insurance. And it is a different measure from the "adjusted income"
and "threshold income" used for the tapered annual allowance, which are
described further down.
:::

## Using your pension to reduce your adjusted net income

If you have followed the [flowchart](/flowchart/) and have money set aside
for an emergency fund and for any short term goals, you are in a good
position to benefit from the tax savings of putting money away in a pension.

Pensions allow you to defer income tax today, and save National Insurance if
the contribution is made by salary sacrifice, until retirement, when you may
withdraw at a lower marginal rate.

You have an annual allowance for pension contributions, and any contribution
within this allowance attracts tax relief at your marginal tax rate. See
[Income Tax - The Basics](/income-tax/) for more on marginal rates. The
annual allowance covers your own contributions, contributions made on your
behalf by someone else, and your employer's contributions.

The annual allowance for 2026/27 is
[£60,000](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/rates-and-allowances-pension-schemes/pension-schemes-rates).

Separately, tax relief on personal contributions is limited to the higher of
100% of your UK taxable earnings or £3,600 gross. So for most people the
practical limit is the lower of £60,000 and their earnings, but an employer
contribution can take the total above your salary.

If your income is over
£200,000 the allowance may be tapered, which is covered under
[Tax Trap 3](#tax-trap-3-tapered-annual-allowance-over-200000-earnings)
below.

You can also [potentially 'carry forward' unused annual allowances](/pensions/#carry-forward)
from the last three tax years. The annual allowance was £60,000 in each of
2023/24, 2024/25 and 2025/26, so for 2026/27 the theoretical maximum with
full carry forward is £240,000, though your own contributions are still
capped at your earnings. You must use the current year's allowance first,
then the earliest unused year, and you must have been a member of a UK
registered pension scheme in each year you carry forward from
([HMRC Pensions Tax Manual PTM055100](https://www.gov.uk/hmrc-internal-manuals/pensions-tax-manual/ptm055100)).

:::warning[Money Purchase Annual Allowance]
One important exception. If you have already flexibly accessed a defined
contribution pension, for example by taking taxable income from drawdown or
an uncrystallised funds pension lump sum, the
[money purchase annual allowance](https://www.gov.uk/tax-on-your-private-pension/annual-allowance)
of £10,000 applies instead, and carry forward cannot be used against it. If
that is you, check the position before making a large contribution.
:::

### Is a pension the right choice?

You may be wondering if it is the right decision to increase pension
contributions if you were not otherwise planning to. We would generally
suggest that people work out their long-term goals first, and decide how
much to save for them as a result. However, in the situations below, the tax
cost of the trap is so high that it makes pension contributions even more
valuable than usual. With that said, please ensure you have enough in
'spare' funds before making any decisions. You should still be at the
"long-term goals" section of [the flowchart](/flowchart/) before considering
this route.

Money in a pension is locked away until at least normal
minimum pension age, which is 55 now and rises to 57 on 6 April 2028.

### Tax relief in the examples: relief at source vs salary sacrifice/net pay {#tax-relief-in-the-examples--relief-at-source-vs-salary-sacrificenet-pay}

Although the way to achieve the full higher-rate tax relief is quite
different in the examples below, the overall effect for the tax traps is the
same.

All SIPPs operate as "relief at source", but workplace pensions may be
relief at source, salary sacrifice, or net pay.

For relief at source, higher-rate and additional-rate relief must be claimed
directly from HMRC. They will only provide basic-rate relief "at source".

If available, salary sacrifice generally tends to be preferable, as you save
National Insurance on the amount, and you do not need to reclaim the
higher-rate tax relief manually.

"Net pay schemes" sit somewhere between the two. You do not need to claim
higher rate tax relief, but they do not provide a National Insurance
reduction.

If you are in doubt over what type of pension scheme your employer operates,
ask them. If they do not know the answer, ask the pension provider.

See our [Pensions](/pensions/) page for further reading, specifically the
section [How do I claim higher rate tax relief?](/pensions/#claiming-tax-relief)

:::caution[Salary sacrifice is changing from April 2029]
From 6 April 2029 only the first **£2,000** a year of pension contributions
made by salary sacrifice will be exempt from National Insurance. Anything
sacrificed above that will attract employee and employer Class 1 National
Insurance. Income tax relief is not affected, and salary sacrifice will
still reduce your adjusted net income, so the trap-avoidance described on
this page still works. Only the National Insurance saving is capped. This is
already law, in the
[National Insurance Contributions (Employer Pensions Contributions) Act 2026](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2026/15/enacted),
with the detail to follow in regulations. See
[HMRC's policy paper](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/salary-sacrifice-reform-for-pension-contributions-effective-from-6-april-2029/salary-sacrifice-reform-for-pension-contributions)
for the full picture. Nothing changes for 2026/27, 2027/28 or 2028/29.
:::

## Tax Trap 1: Removal of Personal Allowance at £100,000 earnings

**Effective marginal income tax rate between £100,000 and £125,140: 60%.**
Including the 2% employee National Insurance charged above the upper
earnings limit, the marginal rate on employment income is 62%.

For every £2 your adjusted net income rises above £100,000, £1 of your
personal allowance is removed. The personal allowance is £12,570, so it is
fully lost once your adjusted net income reaches £125,140
([gov.uk, Income Tax rates and Personal Allowances](https://www.gov.uk/income-tax-rates)).

The arithmetic: an extra £2 of income is taxed at 40%, costing 80p, and it
also exposes another £1 of income that the personal allowance was covering,
costing another 40p. That is £1.20 of tax on £2 of income.

### The childcare cliff edge at £100,000

For parents, £100,000 is worse than a 60% marginal rate, because two
separate childcare schemes are withdrawn entirely at that point. There is no
taper. One pound of extra adjusted net income can cost thousands.

- [Tax-Free Childcare](https://www.gov.uk/tax-free-childcare) pays £2 for
  every £8 you put into the account, capped at £500 per child per quarter,
  so £2,000 per child per year, or £4,000 if the child is disabled. You are
  ineligible if either parent's adjusted net income is over £100,000.
- The [funded childcare hours for working parents in England](https://www.gov.uk/free-childcare-if-working/check-youre-eligible)
  give 30 hours a week for 38 weeks a year for children aged 9 months to 4
  years. The same £100,000 adjusted net income limit applies to each parent.

Both tests are on adjusted net income, so a pension contribution or Gift Aid
donation that brings you back under £100,000 restores both. Note the limit
applies to each parent individually, and one parent going over it
disqualifies the household.

The universal 15 hours a week for all 3 and 4 year olds in England is not
income tested and is unaffected.

### Example: £100k tax trap + Salary Sacrifice pension

You earn £125,140 this year. You sacrifice £25,140 of your salary and your
employer contributes £25,140 to your pension in exchange.

Your taxable pay is now £100,000, you retain the full £12,570 personal
allowance, and you save National Insurance on the sacrificed amount.

Had you not made this change, that £25,140 of gross pay would have produced
only £9,553.20 of take-home pay: £15,084 lost to income tax at an effective
60%, and £502.80 to National Insurance at 2%. Instead the full £25,140 goes
into your pension.

### Example: £100k tax trap + Relief at Source pension

You earn £125,140 and your pension is relief at source rather than salary
sacrifice.

To bring your adjusted net income to £100,000 you need a gross contribution
of £25,140. You pay £20,112 from your bank account and your SIPP or
workplace pension provider reclaims £5,028 of basic rate relief from HMRC on
your behalf.

You then claim the rest through self assessment, or by contacting HMRC. Two
things happen. Your basic rate band is extended by the £25,140 gross
contribution, which is worth a further 20% on that amount. And your personal
allowance is restored in full, so £12,570 that was being taxed at 40% no
longer is. Together those come to £10,056, which HMRC refunds to you.

So in summary:

- The contribution into your pension is £25,140.
- It cost you £20,112 up front, of which HMRC returns £10,056.
- The net cost from your take-home pay is £10,056, which is exactly what the
  same £25,140 of salary would have been worth after 60% tax.
- Unlike salary sacrifice, there is no National Insurance saving.

## Tax Trap 2: Removal of Child Benefit at £60,000 earnings

Child Benefit for 2026/27 is
[£27.05 a week for the eldest or only child and £17.90 a week for each
additional child](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/rates-and-allowances-tax-credits-child-benefit-and-guardians-allowance/tax-credits-child-benefit-and-guardians-allowance).
Over a 52 week year that is £1,406.60 for one child, £2,337.40 for two and
£3,268.20 for three.

Effective marginal rate on income between £60,000 and £80,000:

| Children | Child Benefit a year | Income tax only | Including 2% National Insurance |
| -------- | -------------------- | --------------- | ------------------------------- |
| One      | £1,406.60            | 47.0%           | 49.0%                           |
| Two      | £2,337.40            | 51.7%           | 53.7%                           |
| Three    | £3,268.20            | 56.3%           | 58.3%                           |

These rates change every April, because Child Benefit is uprated and the
£60,000 to £80,000 band is not. Each uprating makes this trap slightly
worse.

You are usually eligible for Child Benefit if you are responsible for a
child [under 16, or under 20 if they stay in approved full-time non-advanced
education or training](https://www.gov.uk/child-benefit/eligibility). A
university degree does not count.

The benefit is progressively withdrawn from parents whose adjusted net
income is over £60,000 through the
[High Income Child Benefit Charge](https://www.gov.uk/child-benefit-tax-charge).
For every £200 earned over £60,000 you repay 1% of the Child Benefit, so
100% is repaid once you reach £80,000. You can work out your own figure with
[HMRC's calculator](https://www.gov.uk/child-benefit-tax-calculator).

The charge is based on individual income, not household income. Two parents
earning £59,000 each keep the full entitlement. One parent earning £80,000
with a partner earning nothing loses all of it. If both are over £60,000,
the charge falls on whoever has the higher adjusted net income.

Note: in tax years up to and including 2023/24 the charge started at £50,000
and tapered over £10,000 rather than £20,000, which produced marginal rates
above 70%. It was then a near automatic decision to divert the income into a
pension. At today's 47% to 56% the maths is less one-sided, and it is worth
weighing the value of the money now against the value of the pension
contribution.

### Example: £60k tax trap + Salary Sacrifice pension

You earn £70,000 and have two children. At that income you repay half of
your Child Benefit, a charge of £1,168.70.

You sacrifice £10,000 of salary into your pension, bringing your adjusted
net income to £60,000, so no charge is due at all.

That £10,000 would have been worth £5,800 in your pocket after 40% income
tax and 2% National Insurance. Avoiding the charge saves a further
£1,168.70. So £10,000 into your pension costs you £4,631.30 of take-home
pay, an effective relief rate of 53.7%.

### Example: £60k tax trap + Relief at Source pension

Same salary, same two children, but a relief at source scheme.

You make a gross contribution of £10,000 by paying £8,000, with your
provider reclaiming £2,000 from HMRC. You then claim a further £2,000 of
higher rate relief through self assessment or by contacting HMRC, and the
charge of £1,168.70 falls away.

The net cost is £4,831.30. That is £200 more than the salary sacrifice
route, which is the 2% National Insurance you do not save.

## Tax Trap 3: Tapered annual allowance over £200,000 earnings

If your income is over £200,000 you may be affected by the tapered annual
allowance, which reduces the amount you can contribute to your pension in a
tax-efficient way. It applies only if **both** of the following are true
([gov.uk](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/pension-schemes-work-out-your-tapered-annual-allowance)):

- Your threshold income is over £200,000.
- Your adjusted income is over £260,000.

For every £2 of adjusted income over £260,000, your annual allowance is
reduced by £1. The minimum it can fall to is £10,000, which on a £60,000
starting allowance means the taper stops biting once adjusted income reaches
£360,000.

Note that "adjusted income" and "threshold income" are not the same as the
"adjusted net income" used elsewhere on this page, and they move in the
opposite direction. Adjusted net income comes **down** when you contribute
to a pension. Adjusted income **adds back** all pension contributions,
including your employer's, so it does not fall when you contribute more.
Threshold income sits in between: it deducts relief at source contributions
but adds back any salary sacrificed for pension since 8 July 2015. HMRC sets
out both calculations step by step on
[the taper guidance page](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/pension-schemes-work-out-your-tapered-annual-allowance).

The practical consequence is that the usual answer of "put it in the
pension" does not work here, because contributing does not reduce adjusted
income. If you exceed the tapered allowance you pay an annual allowance
charge on the excess, so you are taxed on the way in and again on the way
out.

Carry forward can still help. Unused allowance from the previous three years
is available even if you are tapered in the current year, and unused
allowance from a tapered year is carried forward at that year's reduced
figure rather than the headline £60,000.

Further information is on
[MoneyHelper](https://www.moneyhelper.org.uk/en/pensions-and-retirement/tax-and-pensions/tapered-annual-allowance).
We do not provide worked examples here because of the complexity. If you are
affected you should consider seeking [financial advice](/financial-advice/)
or speaking to a tax accountant.

## A note for Scottish taxpayers

Scotland sets its own rates and bands for income tax on earnings, so the
marginal rates above do not apply. For 2026/27 the
[Scottish rates](https://www.gov.uk/scottish-income-tax) are 19% starter, 20%
basic, 21% intermediate, 42% higher from £43,663, 45% advanced from £75,001
and 48% top above £125,140.

Three consequences of this:

- Scottish taxpayers reach the higher rate at £43,663 rather than £50,270,
  so the trap starts earlier. National Insurance thresholds are UK-wide and
  do not move with the Scottish bands, which means there is a slice of income
  taxed at 42% while 8% National Insurance is still being charged.
- In the personal allowance taper band the marginal income tax rate is 67.5%
  rather than 60%, because the 45% advanced rate is being applied one and a
  half times over. With 2% National Insurance that is 69.5%.
- The personal allowance, the £100,000 taper, the £60,000 child benefit
  threshold, National Insurance and the pension annual allowance are all set
  UK-wide and are identical in Scotland. Only the income tax rates and bands
  differ. Dividends and savings interest are also taxed at UK-wide rates.

## Other ways to reduce your adjusted net income

Using your pension is the main method, but there are others available.
Salary sacrifice for various perks is becoming more common, but beware that
these may attract tax known as "Benefit in Kind" (BiK).

Since April 2017 most salary sacrifice benefits are taxed on the higher of
the salary given up and the normal benefit in kind value, which removes the
saving. Only a short list of benefits is exempt from that rule
([HMRC](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/salary-sacrifice-and-the-effects-on-paye)):
pension contributions, employer provided pensions advice, workplace
nurseries, some legacy childcare arrangements, and cycles and cycling safety
equipment. Do not assume your adjusted net income will fall by the full
amount you sacrifice for anything outside that list.

### Cycle to Work scheme (no BiK charge)

Cycle to Work allows you to hire a bike from the scheme provider using a
voucher paid from your gross salary under a salary sacrifice arrangement.
The voucher is typically repaid over 12 months directly out of your payroll.

This reduces your taxable income and the amount of income tax and National
Insurance you pay. Provided the bike is available to all employees and is
mainly used for commuting, there is
[no benefit in kind charge and nothing to report](https://www.gov.uk/expenses-and-benefits-bikes-for-employees).
You do not need to prove that you use the bike for commuting, you only need
to intend to at the point of joining the scheme.

You may see a £1,000 limit quoted. That is not a tax limit. It comes from
consumer credit rules that let an employer hire goods to staff without being
authorised by the FCA, and it does not apply where the employer is FCA
authorised or where a third party scheme provider owns the bike, which is
the usual arrangement
([Department for Transport guidance](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/cycle-to-work-scheme-implementation-guidance)).

Owning and using a bike has many benefits, but it is additional expenditure,
so this should only be used if you can afford the cost and actually intend
to use the bike. Scheme providers have varying rules on how you eventually
come to own the bike.

**Example**

You obtain a £1,200 Cycle to Work voucher, repaid at £100 of gross salary a
month over 12 months.

You earn £125,140 and have no personal allowance. The voucher reduces your
taxable income to £123,940, which saves £480 of income tax at 40%, restores
£600 of personal allowance worth another £240, and saves £24 of National
Insurance. That is £744 of tax and National Insurance saved.

Your take-home pay falls by £456 over the year, and you have a £1,200 bike.

### Buying additional holiday days {#buying-additional-holiday-days-no-bik-charge}

Many large employers offer the option to buy additional holiday. This is
usually treated as a straightforward reduction in pay in exchange for
working fewer hours, rather than as a benefit in kind, so it reduces your
adjusted net income by the full amount.

HMRC does not publish specific guidance on holiday purchase, so if this
matters to your planning, ask your employer to confirm how their scheme is
treated for tax and how it appears on your payslip.

### Salary sacrifice car leasing (BiK charge)

A growing number of employers offer salary sacrifice schemes for leasing new
cars. Because buying or leasing a brand new car is a luxury purchasing
decision, it probably should not be entered into just to reduce your
adjusted net income, but if you would be buying or leasing a new car anyway,
it might be worth considering.

The tax effect is more complicated than the other schemes here. Cars are
normally caught by the April 2017 rule described above, meaning you are
taxed on the greater of the salary given up and the benefit in kind value.
The exception is cars emitting no more than 75g/km of CO2, where the normal
benefit in kind rules apply instead
([HMRC](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/salary-sacrifice-and-the-effects-on-paye)).
That exception is why electric and ultra-low emission cars are the ones
these schemes tend to offer.

The benefit in kind is a percentage of the car's list price, set by its CO2
emissions. For a fully electric car the percentage is
[4% in 2026/27](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/company-car-benefit-the-appropriate-percentage-480-appendix-2),
rising to 5% in 2027/28 and 7% in 2028/29
([HM Treasury](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/income-tax-company-car-tax-rates-2028-to-2030/taxation-of-company-cars-the-appropriate-percentage-for-tax-years-2028-to-2029-and-2029-to-2030)).
Your adjusted net income falls by the salary sacrificed and rises by the
benefit in kind, so the net reduction is smaller than the amount you
sacrifice. The scheme operator should explain the specifics for the car you
are considering.

### Donating to charity / Gift Aid

Donating to a charity can reduce your adjusted net income in a similar way
to pension contributions.

When making a donation, fill in a
[Gift Aid declaration](https://www.gov.uk/donating-to-charity/gift-aid) and
the charity can claim an extra 25p for every £1 you give, which is the basic
rate tax you are treated as having already paid.

As a higher or additional rate taxpayer you can claim the difference through
your self assessment return, or by contacting HMRC if you do not file one.
The gross donation, meaning what you gave plus what the charity reclaimed,
is what comes off your adjusted net income.

#### Gift Aid example

You earn £105,000 this year. You donate £4,000 to a charity and make a Gift
Aid declaration. The charity claims an extra £1,000, so the gross donation
is £5,000.

Your adjusted net income falls from £105,000 to £100,000, restoring the
£2,500 of personal allowance you had lost. Your basic rate band is also
extended by £5,000. Between them those are worth £2,000, which HMRC refunds
to you.

So the charity receives £5,000 and it has cost you £2,000 of net income. The
government has funded £3,000 of the gift, which is 60%, the same effective
rate as the trap itself.

#### Carrying Gift Aid into a previous tax year

You can elect to treat a donation as if it were made in the previous tax
year. This is useful if you were a higher rate taxpayer last year but will
not be this year, or if you want the relief sooner.

The election is only valid if you make it on your return **and file that
return by the normal deadline**, meaning 31 January if you file online or 31
October if you file on paper. A late return prevents the claim. There are
more details at [gov.uk](https://www.gov.uk/donating-to-charity/gift-aid).

#### Payroll Giving

Payroll giving is another way of reducing your income tax liability. You
elect to donate to charity from your gross salary, and the donation is taken
before income tax is calculated, so there is nothing to reclaim afterwards.

Because it is an election rather than a salary sacrifice, you still pay
National Insurance on the amount donated. You do receive income tax relief
at your marginal rate.

**Example**

You earn £105,000 and donate £5,000 through Payroll Giving. Your taxable pay
becomes £100,000, so the £5,000 escapes 40% tax and your full personal
allowance is restored, worth another £1,000. The £5,000 donation costs you
£2,000 of take-home pay. National Insurance is unchanged.

### Tax advantaged investment schemes

There are a handful of tax advantaged schemes for investing in eligible
small UK companies. These come with a significant risk of capital loss, so
the government offers advantageous tax conditions to encourage investors.

Although these schemes provide tax relief, they do **not** reduce your
adjusted net income, so they do not get you out of the tax traps in the way
the other options do. They refund some of the tax you paid, through their own
reliefs.

All three are high risk. You can lose everything you put in. They are not a
substitute for a pension or an ISA, and they sit past the end of
[the flowchart](/flowchart/).

The figures below are from
[HMRC's guidance for investors](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/venture-capital-schemes-tax-relief-for-investors),
updated 6 April 2026.

#### Enterprise Investment Scheme / Seed Enterprise Investment Scheme

SEIS gives 50% income tax relief on up to £200,000 invested a year. EIS gives
30% on up to £1 million, or £2 million if at least £1 million of it goes into
knowledge-intensive companies. Both require the shares to be held for at
least three years, both allow gains on disposal to be free of capital gains
tax where income tax relief was given and retained, and both allow losses to
be set against income. Both also allow the relief to be carried back to the
previous tax year.

These are direct investments in unlisted companies and the risk of total
loss is significant.

#### Venture Capital Trusts

VCTs give **20%** income tax relief on up to £200,000 a year, with a
five-year minimum holding period, and dividends from them are tax free.
Relief can only be claimed in the tax year you invest, with no carry back.

Note that the VCT relief rate was reduced with effect from 6 April 2026.
The income tax relief on both EIS and VCT is currently legislated to run
until [6 April 2035](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/extension-of-the-enterprise-investment-scheme-and-venture-capital-trust-scheme/enterprise-investment-scheme-and-venture-capital-trusts-scheme-extension).

VCTs invest in groups of small unlisted companies and are a very high-risk
investment with a significant risk of capital loss. They also usually have high fees,
and are 'active investments' (see [investing 101](/investing-101/#what-are-passive-and-active-investing-).

## Do I need to complete a Self Assessment?

The old rule that anyone earning over £100,000, and later £150,000, had to
file a return **no longer exists**. The income threshold for people taxed
only through PAYE was removed from the 2024/25 tax year onwards. Being a high earner is not, by itself, a reason to file.

[HMRC's list of reasons you must send a return](https://www.gov.uk/self-assessment-tax-returns/who-must-send-a-tax-return)
now covers people who were self-employed and earned more than £1,000 gross,
partners in a business partnership, people who owe capital gains tax, people
who owe the High Income Child Benefit Charge and are not paying it through
PAYE, and off-payroll workers repaying a student loan. You may also need to
file if you have untaxed income such as rent, tips, savings interest,
dividends or foreign income. If you are not sure, use
[HMRC's checker](https://www.gov.uk/check-if-you-need-tax-return).

### Paying the High Income Child Benefit Charge through your tax code

You no longer have to register for self assessment purely to pay the
charge. HMRC now lets you
[pay it through PAYE instead](https://www.gov.uk/child-benefit-tax-charge/pay-tax-charge-paye),
for the 2025/26 tax year onwards. You can only use this route if you do not
need to file a return for some other reason, and you have to register on or
before 31 January following the tax year concerned. Once registered, HMRC
adjusts your tax code and you do not need to do anything else unless your
circumstances change.

### Notices to file

If HMRC issues you with a notice to file, you must submit a return. Even if
you think you will not owe any additional tax, failure to do so will result
in penalties, including a late filing penalty and interest.

You can ask for the notice to be withdrawn if your affairs are simple and
can be dealt with through PAYE. HMRC's internal guidance allows a withdrawal
request within
[two years of the end of the tax year](https://www.gov.uk/hmrc-internal-manuals/self-assessment-manual/sam120115),
though it is far simpler to ask before the filing deadline. If a notice is
withdrawn, HMRC cancels the associated late filing penalties.

### Telling HMRC about untaxed income

If you have not received a notice to file but you know you owe tax, you must
tell HMRC. This is not necessarily by tax return, and you can contact them
directly if the position is straightforward. Income to watch includes:

- Gains made outside of an [ISA](https://www.gov.uk/individual-savings-accounts)
- [Rental income from properties](https://www.gov.uk/renting-out-a-property/paying-tax) or [the rent-a-room scheme](https://www.gov.uk/rent-room-in-your-home/the-rent-a-room-scheme)
- [Savings interest](https://www.gov.uk/apply-tax-free-interest-on-savings)
- [Dividends](https://www.gov.uk/tax-on-dividends)

This is not an exhaustive list. If you have any other taxable income, HMRC
must be informed. If you are unsure, seek professional tax advice.

Two changes worth noting if you hold investments outside an ISA. Dividend
tax rates rose by two percentage points from 6 April 2026, to 10.75% for
basic rate and 35.75% for higher rate taxpayers, with the additional rate
unchanged at 39.35%. Savings and property income rates rise by two
percentage points from 6 April 2027, to 22%, 42% and 47%
([HMRC technical note, 26 November 2025](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/changes-to-tax-rates-for-property-savings-and-dividend-income/change-to-tax-rates-for-property-savings-and-dividend-income-technical-note)).
Both make the ISA and pension wrappers relatively more valuable.

## Should I claim child benefit if I have to pay it back?

If you earn between £60,000 and £80,000 and cannot reduce your income
further by tax efficient means, you are still better off claiming child
benefit even though you will repay some of it. You could set the money aside
in a savings account until the charge falls due and keep the interest.

If your income is above £80,000 even after all salary sacrifice, pension
contributions and reliefs, you should still register for child benefit but
opt out of receiving the payments.

This is because a parent or guardian
[registered for child benefit for a child under 12 receives Class 3 National
Insurance credits](https://www.gov.uk/national-insurance-credits/eligibility),
whether or not the money is actually paid. Those credits can turn a year
into a qualifying year for the state pension, which matters if one parent
stays at home or works too few hours to build a qualifying year through
National Insurance contributions.

Registering also means your child is
[automatically issued a National Insurance number in the three months before
they turn 16](https://www.gov.uk/apply-national-insurance-number), rather
than having to apply for one. And if your circumstances change so that you
become entitled to keep some or all of the money, you can start receiving it
faster if you are already registered.
